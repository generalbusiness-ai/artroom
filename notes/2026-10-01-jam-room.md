## Summary

- **What it is.** Several agent musicians and one human, perhaps playing a
  MIDI keyboard, improvise together in an Artroom room. It is a
  side-demonstration, with a short clip in the contest video.
- **Why.** The code-change workflow hides how general Artroom is. A jam is
  freeform, social and has nothing to do with pull requests. If the same
  platform steps and policy runtime can enforce the jam's own declared
  acts, the generality is shown through the application.
- **The design rule.** The room records commitments: who plays which part,
  who has the solo, the song's key and tempo, the patterns, the samples and
  the takes. Playing itself, which is notes, knob movements and cursors,
  travels in a separate live layer that is never authoritative.
- **Timing.** A committed change takes effect at the first bar boundary at
  least one lookahead after the room admitted it. Commit latency becomes
  lookahead, not jitter, and every listener and every later replay computes
  the same bar.
- **A separate application.** The jam lives in its own repository and
  depends on Artroom only through published packages and a deployed room.
  It is the worked instance for deciding how applications are distributed
  (section 9).
- **Acceptance criterion: no platform code in jam.** Jam uses published
  Artroom packages and a deployed room. A platform gap is a finding and
  a separate request in Artroom's workroom. Artroom may continue to improve
  while jam is built; the application does not patch or import its source.
- **The first distribution finding.** The room reaches checkers over
  service bindings fixed in its own deployment, so an application's checker
  cannot be reached without redeploying the platform. Section 9 lists the
  options, and a way for the jam to avoid the question.
- **The open question is musical, not technical.** Whether agents can play
  together well enough to be worth hearing is unknown. Spike J2 is the
  bounded way to test that question when it blocks the musical build.

## 1. Why a jam

Earlier candidates for a second demonstration (a shared mural, an editorial
desk) were still git-shaped: claim, propose, review, land. A jam is not. It
is mostly live motion, punctuated by a few moments that matter: who takes
the next solo, when the band goes back to the head, a change of key, a new
loop. Jazz players coordinate those moments with an agreed form and a few
hand signals. The declared-acts design gives those moments their own
names, typed bodies and thread restrictions. Its section 7 is the
starting musical fixture; these are planned semantics, not a claim that
every primitive is already deployed:

| In a jam | Jam declaration and platform steps |
|---|---|
| "I've got the bass" | `take-part`: `open` over fixed `parts/{part}/**`, exclusive, with a workspace |
| taking a solo | `take-solo`: `open` or `take`, only on solo threads, over fixed `solo/**` |
| pointing to the next soloist | `pass-solo`: `hand-over`, reserving the solo for the named active member |
| a late entry over someone else's solo | refused `lane-held`, `reserved` or `scope-overlap` as applicable, with the declaration's reason and fix |
| a player who drops out | the thread's recorded lease ends; musical turns normally end deliberately |
| tap head, fist (ending), "one more" | `signal`: `comment`, with typed signal and optional member, with or without an entry anchor |
| a new loop or voicing | `add-pattern`: `version` then `land` on a part thread, with check-only obligations; separate version/review/land when reviews are required |
| the key and tempo | `song.json`; `lead` holds that scope and `change-key` acts only on its thread |
| house rules | `propose-rules`, `approve-rules`, `adopt-rules`; an admin's approval and exact-sequence activation |
| the recording | `takes/**`, committed by each player |

**Which acts need stage 4 before this musical session.** `take-part`,
`lead` and `take-solo` need the new exclusive conflict checks; `take-part`
also needs fixed scope slots. All three use fixed scopes; the solo's
hold also omits a workspace. Stage 2 owns recorded thread kinds,
bindings and the lease rule; stage 4 adds conflict and reservation
settings and fixed scopes. `pass-solo`
needs reserved hand-over and reservation-end events. An unanchored
`signal` needs the new `comment` target. `add-pattern` and `change-key`
need compound `version` then `land`; their automated checks also need
v2 jobs that name the declared check kind and binding. Stage 4 owns these
new capabilities and their migration and race tests. Typed declarations,
binding-aware admission, replay and generic clients come from the other
stages. This musical-session list does not set the start threshold for
building the application or its development workroom.

The woo `dubspace` demo (`~/play/woo/catalogs/dubspace/`) is the direct
ancestor. It already separates live slider previews, which are never
sequenced, from committed control changes, which are. The jam room keeps
that split and puts the committed half in an Artroom room.

## 2. The timing model

### Three clocks

1. **The audio clock.** Local to each listener and exact to the sample.
   Web Audio schedules against it.
2. **The musical clock.** Bars and beats, computed from the historical
   tempo segments and a recorded transport start.
3. **The commit clock.** The room's sequence. Every act carries the room's
   admission time, `at` (`packages/contract/src/acts.ts`).

### The rule

A committed change takes effect at **the first bar boundary at least one
lookahead after its admission time**. For a landing, the admission time is
that of the room's `landed` receipt. Each transport run starts with an
**unanchored** declared `signal`, with target `none` and body signal
`count-in`. This log entry's ID and admitted `at` record the origin of bar 1.
An entry-anchored count-in remains commentary and cannot start or reset the
transport. A later unanchored count-in records a new run; it does not erase
the earlier one.

Before a run starts, the leader lands its initial `song.json`: tempo,
meter, form and positive lookahead values in milliseconds, separately for
signals/solos and landed patterns. The starting count-in uses that landed
file version. Four beats per bar is the meter for the numerical examples
below. A listener's local audio buffer is not a scheduling input.

Process the log in `seq` order, retaining the file versions named by
landing receipts. For each change, use the current transport run and the
lookahead configuration in effect at its admission time. Use the tempo
schedule established by lower-sequence entries, including scheduled
segments that have not started yet. Find the first boundary at or after
`at + lookaheadMs` on that schedule and retain its bar number as that
change's effect bar. Resolve changes on the same bar in `seq`
order. A tempo or lookahead change uses the preceding configuration to
choose its own effect bar, then activates there. A tempo change starts a
new segment at that bar's existing boundary. It can change the duration
of future bars, but cannot renumber an earlier assigned effect bar or
rewrite an elapsed boundary. Retain the old segments and configurations;
never calculate earlier changes from the latest `song.json` or a
listener's current settings.

The committed inputs are the origin entry, admission/landing times and
exact `song.json` versions in the log and repository history.
Activation bars are derived by this ordered calculation,
not chosen from a listener's arrival time. Every listener, and a replay
from a fresh clone with the full log and referenced file history, therefore
assigns the same effect bars. The eventual musical fixture must compare
live processing with fresh-clone replay across both a tempo change and a
lookahead/configuration change, including an already scheduled effect.
This tests the existing replay claim; it adds no platform commission or
development-start gate.

Listeners estimate their offset from the room clock through the live
layer, in the usual round-trip way.

A listener that receives a change after its effect bar has already passed
plays it late. Its live audio differs briefly from other listeners'. The
record does not, and the stage client (section 6) renders the official mix.

### Structure is committed, playing is live

The room does not need an act for each turn. The band agrees a form in
advance, for example "trade fours: sax, keys, drums, from bar 65", and the
form is a committed file. Turns then follow the musical clock. Acts mark
departures from the plan: a signal, a new soloist, an ending.

### Choose forms that tolerate latency

Call and response, trading, dub and modal vamps all tolerate a reply that
arrives one bar later; it sounds intended. Tight unison comping does not.
The jam uses the first kind.

### Precedents

- [NINJAM](https://www.cockos.com/ninjam/) accepts network latency by
  having each player hear the others one interval late, and play over that.
- [Ableton Link](https://ableton.github.io/link/) coordinates tempo,
  beat alignment and phase among independent device timelines.

These are precedents for coordinating musical time, the shared reference
in the jam's timing rule above.

### What the numbers allow

**Historical measurements** from the room-core and sandbox-git spikes
(EWR, warm), retained as inputs to J1, not fresh declared-acts timings:

- a legacy act with no diff (`claim`, `note`, `release`): 68–73 ms p50;
- `propose` of a one-file change: 305 ms p50;
- a merge preview: about 210 ms; a landing: about 510 ms inside the
  Durable Object;
- the first container start after a deploy: 5.9 s; later starts 223–675 ms.

**Not measured:** delivery from the room to a listener through the chosen
update transport, a full pattern change from push to `landed`, and landing
throughput with many players. The plan's targets are a live update within
1 s and at least 10 disjoint landings a minute.

At 120 bpm a bar lasts 2 s. A one-bar lookahead for signals and solos is
a hypothesis. A pattern change needs a push, version admission, validator
evidence and a landing receipt, even if one declared act requests version
and landing together. Two or three bars may be acceptable for loops and
voicings; J1 measures both paths on the deployed version actually used.

## 3. What lives where

### Room acts

- **Parts.** `take-part` takes a fixed exclusive hold on
  `parts/<part>/**`, with a workspace. Its recorded lease settings belong
  to that thread for its life; `leave` releases a part or lead thread.
- **Solos.** `take-solo` opens or takes only a solo thread. `pass-solo`
  reserves it for one named active member. A fresh solo thread cannot
  bypass a held or reserved exclusive scope; a part thread cannot become
  a solo thread or acquire the solo's scope.
- **Signals.** `signal` has a typed enum (`count-in`, `head`, `ending`,
  `one-more`, `next`) and an optional member. It can stand alone or cite
  an entry. The app shows those values as symbols without inventing a
  parser for free text.
- **House rules and declarations.** The v2 policy file. Changing it
  touches `.artroom/**`, requires an admin-approved landing, and
  activates at an exact sequence number. Actors discover the declaration
  and sign or submit its explicit binding. A changed meaning refuses an
  old intent with `binding-stale`; the actor reads it and deliberately
  resubmits. Historical records keep the declaration of their own seq.

### Repository files

| Path | What it holds | Who lands it |
|---|---|---|
| `song.json` | key, tempo, meter, form and lookahead configuration; historical versions supply the timing inputs in section 2 | the leader, under policy |
| `parts/<part>/*.json` | patterns, voicings, loop choices, sample references | that part's player |
| `samples/<pack>/**` | audio and its provenance record | whoever adds the pack |
| `samples/<pack>/descriptors.json` | pitch, tempo, length, tags | written by the sample checker (section 4) |
| `takes/<player>/<section>.json` | that player's notes for one section | that player |
| `.artroom/policy.json` | act declarations and house rules | through an admin-approved landing |

The transport origin is the unanchored count-in's log entry, rather than a
mutable start-time field in the latest `song.json`. Replay retains each
run's origin and the file versions and activation bars derived in section 2.

Each player commits their own takes, so the recording is attributed and
signed part by part. Nothing in the record is authored on anyone's behalf
by the stage. The starter fixture also needs scoped declarations for
`takes/<player>/**`, with authority for that player's files. A part-only
thread over `parts/<part>/**` cannot commit a take outside that scope.

### The live layer

A separate Durable Object in the jam app's own Worker, using WebSocket
hibernation. It carries notes being played, knob drags, cursors, per-part
energy and density figures, and clock-offset pings. Every message carries
musical time.

The rule: **nothing in the live layer is authoritative.** Anything that
matters becomes an act or a committed file. This is the same rule as for
the K2 exporter in request `da2737b2`: other systems carry copies, never
authority.

The live layer is part of the jam app, not of Artroom. It needs no change
to the platform.

### Committing from a browser or an agent

A player's scoped thread has a workspace: an Artifacts fork and a token
scoped to it (R-WS). The jam app's Worker commits pattern or take files with
isomorphic-git and pushes to that fork, then submits declared version and
landing acts through the generic client. The musical
fixture uses `add-pattern` (`version` then `land`) for check-only
obligations; a version that owes an independent review must be proposed,
reviewed and landed in separate acts. The historical sandbox-git spike found isomorphic-git
works in a plain Worker for small repositories (693 ms p50 for a preview on
its small repository). Samples are larger and go through the same path less
often.

## 4. The sample library

The sample library is the part of the jam that uses the git flow most
directly.

- **Adding a pack.** Open a scoped workspace thread on
  `samples/<pack>/**`, push, version and land through declared acts. The
  starter fixture needs declarations for this path as well as the musical
  part threads; the build must not silently reuse a part-only act.
- **The sample checker.** A `require` rule on `samples/**` asks for a
  `samples` check. The checker runs in a Worker; it executes no repository
  code, so it needs no container. It checks:
  - the format, sample rate, length and peak level;
  - **a provenance record** for every file: who made it, how, and under
    what licence. A file without one fails.

  Section 9 explains why the jam may run this validator as a reviewer
  rather than a checker; what it checks is the same.

  It writes descriptors (pitch, tempo, length, tags) into its result. They
  are how the agents in this design perceive samples symbolically.
  Where the descriptors live is for the build to decide. Either a follow-up
  commit lands them as `descriptors.json`, or the agents read them from the
  check's detail.
- **Exact versions.** A pattern refers to a sample by its blob hash. It
  always plays the version it was written against. Replacing a sample is a
  new blob, and a pattern's review can declare `dependsOn: samples/<pack>/**`
  so that its approval does not carry past the change (R-CARRY-2).
- **Limits recorded in the original plan:** 32 MB per file or blob and
  1 GB per repository. The later log-object amendment records an exact
  32 MiB Git-object bound. History keeps every version. The build must
  check the deployed provider's current limits and size its sample library
  against them; these dated figures are not a fresh provider measurement.
- **Fetching.** Browsers fetch sample blobs through the jam app's Worker,
  cached by hash. No browser holds a token for the canonical repository.

## 5. The agent harness

### Perception is symbolic

This harness gives agents symbolic input. Before each phrase an agent receives:

- the bar and beat position, and the bars until the next form boundary;
- the current chord and key;
- the last N bars of every part, as notes;
- an energy and density figure for each part;
- who holds each part and the solo, and when each lease was last renewed;
- recent signals;
- the available samples, by descriptor.

### Two loops per agent

1. **The phrase loop** writes the next two to four bars before they are
   due. It is a fast loop. It may be a small model, or an algorithm whose
   parameters (density, register, rhythm) the agent steers.
2. **The bandmate loop** is slower. It decides when to take the solo, lay
   out, signal, propose a new pattern or suggest a key change. It acts
   through the room.

A model's response time is then one more lookahead, like commit latency.
Notes are not generated in real time.

### Connected directly

Each player is a long-lived agent holding WebSockets to the live layer and
to the room's `watch` route (R-API-12). `subscribe` instead offers an RPC
byte stream or an HTTPS long poll (R-API-8). Cloudflare's
[Agents SDK](https://developers.cloudflare.com/agents/runtime/communication/websockets/)
supports persistent agents and WebSocket connections and remains a harness
candidate. The musical player uses continuous updates for clock and note
events. MCP's tools and attention reads serve development agents; this
note does not adopt a harness or claim measured musical performance from it.

Each agent signs room acts under a delegation (R-ADM-5), so every act is
attributed to that player.

### Rate and cost

At 120 bpm, a four-bar phrase lasts 8 s, so each agent's phrase loop calls
a model about 7.5 times a minute. Eight agents make about 60 calls a minute,
plus the slower bandmate loops. Each prompt has a large fixed part (the
form, the agent's role, the rules), which may benefit from the chosen
provider's prompt caching. Cost per minute is not estimated here; spike
J2 measures it.

## 6. The human, the MIDI keyboard and the stage

- **Input.** The browser's Web MIDI API reads the keyboard. Notes go to the
  live layer, stamped with musical time.
- **Monitoring.** The human hears their own instrument directly, and the
  band scheduled locally against the shared musical clock.
- **The agents' side.** Agents perceive the human's notes symbolically, one
  bar later. That suits call and response.
- **The human's acts.** The human takes a part, takes or passes a solo and
  signals through UI controls for the same declarations, signed with the
  browser's key and explicit bindings.
- **The stage client** is one listener that renders the official mix for
  the video. It renders from note events and committed files, not from
  captured audio, so the session can be rendered again from the repository.
  Re-rendering requires every performance in the official mix to be
  committed as take data. A missing take is a reported gap; transient live
  previews alone cannot reproduce a session from a fresh clone.
- **Audio.** Web Audio synthesis and committed samples. The woo dubspace
  audio engine is a candidate to reuse; it is our own MIT-licensed code.

## 7. Where the room is audible or visible

If the room only keeps a log, the demonstration does not show Artroom. Four
moments make it visible:

1. **A refused entry.** An agent tries to take the solo while the sax holds
   it. The room refuses according to target and state (`lane-held` or
   `scope-overlap`), with the declaration's wording: "sax has the solo;
   signal to take the next". During hand-over, an unnamed player is refused
   `reserved`; only the named active member can take it before the
   reservation ends. The agents use the refusal to coordinate the next turn.
2. **A key change.** The leader lands a change to `song.json`. Every
   `in-key` check reads `song.json`, and the house rules also list it as a
   global input (R-CARRY-3), so no `in-key` check carries past the change.
   Every open pattern proposal's check reruns and some fail. A `notify` rule puts every player's attention on the change,
   and players whose landed parts no longer fit re-voice them. Listeners
   hear the band move key over two or three bars.
3. **A house rule changes mid-session.** For example, "only the drummer
   lands tempo changes". The policy activates at an exact sequence number.
   A tempo change from the keys player is then refused, while `explain` on
   an earlier tempo change still cites the old rules.
   A changed declaration binding refuses a pending old intent; a label or
   help-only edit leaves its binding unchanged. Historical rendering still
   uses the earlier label and fields at each record's own sequence.
4. **The replay.** After the session, `artroom verify` on a fresh clone
   checks the published log, and the stage client renders the whole jam
   again from the repository.

### Load the jam puts on landing

Landings are serialized per room. Eight players each landing a take every
section, plus patterns, could exceed the unmeasured target of 10 disjoint
landings a minute. So takes are committed per section, not per chorus, and
section length is set from spike J1's measured landing rate. Committing all
takes at the end is the fallback.

### Application declarations and remaining platform findings

The original note bent the jam around seven fixed verbs. Declared acts
remove those vocabulary limits; the platform still enforces its fixed
steps and invariants. The musical fixture is a test of those boundaries:

| Pressure | Planned declared-acts behavior or continuing finding |
|---|---|
| Signals need their own typed body and may have no anchor | `signal` declares its fields and `comment` targets |
| Parts and solos need different leases and holds | Opening declarations set thread-specific leases, fixed scopes and conflict modes; recorded settings survive later declaration changes |
| A solo must be passed to one player without a race | `hand-over` reserves the solo for that member; opening another thread cannot bypass its reserved scope |
| `notify` targets are fixed roles, `owners`, `reviewers` and `holder` | A key change notifies `role:agent` and the human's role |
| Browsers cannot run git directly | The jam app's Worker commits with isomorphic-git |
| Automated check dispatch uses the platform's service bindings | Independent reviewers avoid that coupling where separate version/review/land acts suffice; the full automated musical fixture remains owed (section 9) |

The platform's full musical fixture remains owed: cross-thread refusal,
reservation bypass, overlapping conflict modes, fixed scopes, leader-only
authority, the exact prepared/job/check/landing path, checker-mapping
failures and stale bindings. Section 7.4 of the declared-acts design is
the complete acceptance list. Its eventual proof is distinct from the
builder's earlier judgment that development can start.

If one of these proves insufficient, the build records a platform finding
and files a separate Artroom request. Jam keeps using the published
interface; a later platform release may address the finding.

## 8. Risks

- **Musicianship is unproven.** Agents playing together from symbolic
  perception may sound aimless. This is the largest risk, and spike J2
  tests it when that uncertainty blocks the musical build. The fallback
  is agents steering parameterised generators
  rather than writing notes. The coordination would still be real; the
  music would be simpler.
- **What is heard in the video.** Dependencies of the jam need only a
  licence compatible with Apache 2.0. The tunes and samples heard in the
  video are content, so they are our own: original tunes and changes (a
  12-bar blues or a modal vamp is a form, a standard's melody is not ours),
  and samples we made.
- **Timing.** If delivery to listeners is slower than one bar, signals feel
  late. Spike J1 measures this.
- **Schedule.** The original plan names gate 1 as 2026-10-05 and submission as
  2026-10-13. The live layer, the harness, the audio client and MIDI input
  are several days of work. The jam is built in its own repository and
  takes no core lane, but it competes for planning and review attention.
  J2 needs no room. Current ordering is acts, then spikes that actually
  unblock jam, then jam and Artroom docs together. Those dates do not
  waive scope or require a premature start.
- **Platform faults during a recording.** The original Artroom plan
  records about 5 in 70 repository or fork creations failing with error
  10400 and succeeding on retry. The later sandbox-git spike saw none.
  Lanes and
  workspaces are created before recording, not during it.

## 9. A separate application, and how applications are distributed

The jam is built in its own repository, `~/play/artroom-jam`, published at
<https://github.com/generalbusiness-ai/artroom-jam>. Artroom never depends
on it. It
uses Artroom the way any outside application would, so it shows where the
boundary between the platform and an application really is.

### What an application consists of

| Part | In the jam | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| A room template | the seed tree (`song.json`, the `parts/` layout), v2 declarations and rules in `.artroom/policy.json`, checker configurations when automated checks are used | files |
| Validators | the sample checker and the `in-key` check | Workers holding a key |
| The application Worker | static UI, the live layer's Durable Object, the commit helper | a Worker in the application owner's account |
| Agents | the players | long-lived agents, each signing under its own delegation |
| A setup script | founds a room, lands the template, invites players, sets secrets | a script using only the published client |

### What it may depend on

Only published packages: the contract types and their `/client`,
`/checker` and `/policy` entry points, and the actual client runtime
package. And the URL of a deployed room. No
imports from the Artroom repository's source paths, and no workspace
links. The test is simple: the jam's lockfile resolves every Artroom
package from a registry, pinned to versions that support the chosen
declared-acts interface. Available published versions are an integration
dependency for code that imports them, not a requirement to complete
every unrelated package before the first development task. Builder's
readiness judgment covers the actual task and its dependencies.

### Installing is ordinary acts

No new mechanism is needed to put an application into a room:

1. **Found** a room with repository source `new` (R-GEN-12). The
   repository starts empty.
2. **Land the template** through the founding room's supported bootstrap
   workflow, then activate the application's v2 declarations.
   It touches `.artroom/**`, so it needs an admin's approval. A sole
   admin's own approval is allowed and flagged (R-ADMIN-2). The policy
   activates at the next sequence number (R-POL-9).
3. **Invite** the players, and grant delegations to the application's
   service keys (R-CRED-4).

Upgrading is an admin-approved proposal to `.artroom/**` plus a redeploy of
the application's Workers. Removing is revoking its roster entries and
landing the removal of its files. All of it is in the log, and the platform
needs no registry of applications. This is the same shape as attaching a
dap package at an exact boundary.

### The first finding: how the room reaches an application's checker

The protocol says that the room sends a `CheckJob` to the checker service,
but not how. Lane G sends it over a **service binding** to named
entrypoints (`packages/checkers/README.md` at main `a42c4d83`).
Service bindings are fixed in the room Worker's deployment configuration,
within one Cloudflare account. So an application's checker can be reached
only if the platform's deployment is changed to bind it and redeployed. The
application becomes a deployment dependency of the platform, even though it
is not a code dependency.

Options for the platform. This note adopts none.

| Option | How | Contract change |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Same account | The operator binds each application checker and redeploys | None; couples deployments |
| (b) Endpoint in configuration | `.artroom/checkers/<name>.json` names an HTTPS endpoint; the room sends a job signed with the room key; the checker verifies it | Yes: configuration schema, job authentication, and sending a read token to an outside party |
| (c) Pull | The checker sees the obligation in its attention queue and asks for the job with a signed, unrecorded request, as a workspace is requested (R-CRED-5) | Yes: a new request kind |

**How the jam can avoid the question.** The jam's validators run no
repository code, so they do not need a checker's isolation. They can be
**reviewers**: members or agents holding keys, put in a team, and named by
a `require` rule with a review obligation, for example `parts/**` by
`@harmony`. The validator must be independent of the holder/proposer and
its role must permit review; the checker roster role does not. Reviews are signed
acts over the ordinary transports, so this works across accounts today.
Carrying works the same way for verdicts as for checks (R-CARRY-1 to
R-CARRY-3), so the key change of section 7 still reopens every `in-key`
verdict. With reviews, pattern changes use separate version and landing
acts: `version` then `land` in one act cannot admit a new version and
obtain an independent review of it before that act is admitted. The full
declared-acts musical fixture instead keeps its automated check path,
including prepared events, job kind/binding and mapping failures.
The difference is in meaning: a review is an actor's judgement,
and a check is a machine result with an isolated runner. A real test
runner from an application would still need (b) or (c).

The finding goes to the platform as its own request. The independent
review path lets the initial build proceed without choosing an automated
dispatch option. Builder still judges the dependencies of each actual
task; the full automated musical fixture remains owed.

### Other questions the jam settles

1. **Packages.** Their publication and scope name (decision 6).
2. **Transport.** R-CRED-4 describes a Worker's delegate key over a service
   binding. An application's Worker in another account signs the same
   envelopes over HTTPS. The client should confirm this works without a
   service binding.
3. **Reading a proposal's files.** A reviewer bot must read the head it
   judges. Whether a member's read session covers file contents in the
   canonical repository, or only the log, is to be confirmed.
4. **The commit helper holds a player's workspace token.** The browser gets
   the token as lane holder and hands it to the application's Worker, which
   pushes for it. That gives the application write access to the player's
   fork for the lease. It is acceptable if the UI says so. The alternative
   is git from the browser, if Artifacts accepts cross-origin requests,
   which is unknown.
5. **Versions.** The application pins the contract package version. The
   room's genesis pins the policy profile and `jsonata` version, and a
   mismatch is refused at founding (R-GEN-10). The application says which
   protocol version it speaks.
6. **Where the application's own work is tracked.** In an Artroom room for
   the jam repository, once Artroom is self-hostable: a deployed Artroom
   hosts the jam's requests, proposals and reviews. Platform findings are
   filed in Artroom's workroom as requests.

### Starting development and evolving the workroom

Builder makes the dated readiness judgment against a concrete first task.
The current candidate task is to found a jam development room with v2
workroom declarations and land the jam's declarations and acceptance
cases through generic acts and independent reviews. Development work and
a musical session are different uses of the platform. The first task
does not need the stage-4 automated checker service to understand v2 jobs;
that support and the full musical fixture remain owed later.

Jam's musical room uses its own declarations. Its development workroom
also has an application vocabulary, which may change as self-hosting
reveals better ways to request, promise, review and account for work.
Such changes are reviewed configuration changes at an exact activation
sequence. Pending intents and grants retain their signed bindings; a
client never silently substitutes the new meaning. Historical records
remain readable under their original declarations, including when a
name is retired or reused.

Platform findings return to this Artroom gitseq workroom as separate
requests. Existing platform backlog items continue alongside jam when
capacity permits. Documentation work starts beside jam after a positive
builder judgment, following the complete approved documentation plan.

## 10. Two spikes

Each spike is one request when commissioned. Neither is filed by this
note. They are specific experiments with their original claims and stop
rules, not automatic prerequisites for starting jam development. Builder
identifies which uncertainty blocks the actual next task. Acts have first
priority, then those blocking spikes; jam and docs proceed together.
Work on a spike may start in the jam repository before Artroom is
self-hostable, but its review waits until the jam's room exists (assert
`fdb08e72`). Each freezes its claims as literal expectations before it runs,
and reports when it ends, whatever the result.

### J1: commit-to-effect timing on the deployed room

**When.** When commit-to-effect uncertainty blocks the musical integration,
against the deployed version that the application will use, because it
needs a real landing receipt. Record that version and declaration digest.

**Measured,** with 4 and then 8 simulated players at 120 bpm, reporting
sample counts, p50 and p99, retries, and cold or warm state:

- (a) delivery of a single declared act (part/solo/signal/release) from admission
  to a listener through the chosen update transport, `watch` for the player
  design above. Record that transport; a separate `subscribe` run must name
  RPC or HTTPS and report its results separately;
- (b) a pattern change from push to the `landed` receipt, including the
  check;
- (c) landing throughput when every player lands a pattern every 8 bars;
- (d) the error of a listener's offset estimate against the room clock.

**Claims:**

- (a) p99 is at most 1.5 s, so a one-bar lookahead at 120 bpm holds for
  signals and solos;
- (b) p99 is at most 4 bars (8 s) at that landing rate;
- (d) the offset error is at most 20 ms p99.

**Stop rule.** If (a) fails, the tested one-bar musical demonstration
stops and the finding is reported. Development and the independent
builder readiness judgment are not thereby cancelled. A revised musical
mode needs its own stated timing expectations; a failed claim is not
renamed as success. If only (b) or (c) fails, pattern changes
become section-level, every 16 bars, and the jam continues in that mode.

### J2: agents trading fours

**When.** When the musical feasibility question blocks the next jam task,
in the jam's own repository. It needs no room: a local
harness with a simulated musical clock and the perception of section 5.

**Set-up,** frozen before the run: three or four agents (bass, keys, drums
and one soloist) over an original two-chord modal vamp at 100 bpm. The form
is 8 bars of groove, then 32 bars of trading fours in a fixed order, then 8
bars of groove and an ending. The phrase and bandmate loops are as in
section 5. Output is note events, rendered to audio. An optional second run
has Hugh trading fours on a MIDI keyboard.

**Claims:**

- at least 95% of phrases are delivered before their start bar;
- at least 90% of solo turns start and end on the bars the form names;
- the ending happens on the bar the form names;
- calls per minute and cost per minute are recorded.

**Judgement.** Hugh listens to the rendered audio and answers one question:
would this go in the video? The answer is recorded with the audio.

**Stop rule.** A budget of two revisions to the harness and prompts. If the
answer is still no, the proposed musical demonstration stops, or continues
only in the fallback mode of section 8. This is a musical judgment, not a
gate requiring Hugh to authorize jam development. Missing the timing
claims is reported separately from the musical judgment.
