## Summary

- **What it is.** A person at a microphone hums a tune of a few seconds,
  then mouths or taps a rhythm. An agent at a synth works out the tune and
  plays it back as a track. More agents arrive and each takes an
  instrument. The band answers at about 120 beats a minute. The person
  hums again and the band picks the new phrase up. It is a
  side-demonstration, meant to be funny to watch and to hear.
- **Why.** A code change hides how general Artroom is. A jam is live,
  social and has nothing to do with a pull request. If the same substrate
  enforces the jam's own declared acts, the generality is shown through
  the application.
- **The design rule, kept.** The room records commitments and facts: the
  theme the person sang, the synth's interpretation of it, who holds which
  instrument, each pattern, each solo and the banter. Playing itself
  travels in a separate live layer that is never authoritative.
- **Timing, kept.** A recorded change takes effect at the first bar
  boundary at least one lookahead after the room recorded it. Latency
  becomes lookahead, not jitter, and every listener and every later replay
  computes the same bar.
- **A separate application.** The jam lives in its own repository and
  depends on Artroom only through published packages and a deployed
  service. Neither exists yet (section 7).
- **Acceptance criterion: no platform code in jam.** A platform gap is a
  finding and a separate request in Artroom's workroom. The application
  does not patch or import the platform's source.
- **Two open questions, both for a person's ears.** Whether a synth
  playing back a transcription is recognizably the tune that was hummed
  (spike J0), and whether the band is worth hearing (spike J2). Neither is
  answered.

In this note "the room" means the jam's scope: one scope under the jam's
own definition, with the membership scope of its repository. Section 3
says what that is expected to be on main.

## 1. The story

*Revision 6: rewritten. It replaces "Why a jam".*

Plan 021 states the story:

> A person at a microphone, late at night, hums or sings a few bars,
> "kinda". An agent at a synth listens, works out the tune, and plays it
> back as a track. More agents arrive and each picks up an instrument: the
> first is always the synth, the second the bass line, the third
> percussion, and then a guitar or a saxophone solos over the top. The
> person hums another phrase and the band picks it up; and again, as long
> as they like. The agents talk while they play. It is funny to watch and
> to listen to.

### The reference clip sets the order

The story comes from one 18-second comedy sketch: a singer hums at a
keyboard player, who answers with a finished track. Plan 021 records what
the planner read from the clip on 2026-10-05, from its frames, its
loudness per second, a spectrogram and an onset autocorrelation. Nobody
listened to it for that reading.

| When in the clip | What happens | How the figure was taken |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3.3 s | A voiced hum of about eight syllables | Spike J0's pitch tracker found 8 notes, all between A2 and A sharp 3 (one observed run on this clip; section 10) |
| 3.3 to 7.5 s | Mouth percussion, dry and regular, about four hits a second | The planner's reading; spike J0's onset detector found 18 hits in a 4.2 s excerpt, 4.1 a second (observed run) |
| 7.5 s to the end | The keyboard player's track, at about 120 beats a minute, bass under a synth lead | Onset autocorrelation. Spike J0 notes that the autocorrelation alone does not pick the beat: 120 holds if the 0.25 s gap is an eighth note |

So the clip's shape is tune, then rhythm, then the band, in under twenty
seconds. The joke is that the hum was bad and the track is good. The
demonstration keeps that shape and that length for its first phrase.

### What the person does

1. Hums or sings a tune of a few seconds into the microphone.
2. Mouths or taps a rhythm.
3. Sees each transcription and may sing again if it is wrong.
4. Later hums another phrase, as often as they like.

### What the agents do

1. The first agent takes the synth. It reads the theme, fixes the key,
   the tempo, the time signature and the bar grid, and plays the tune
   back. That is the part a band leader does by counting in.
2. Each agent that arrives takes the next instrument in a fixed order:
   synth, bass, drums, then a lead, which is a guitar or a saxophone.
3. Each player writes patterns that follow the interpretation. The lead
   claims a stretch of bars and solos over the top.
4. They talk while they play.
5. When the person hums a new phrase, the synth interprets it and the
   band changes to it at a bar boundary.

### What a watcher sees and hears

A white room, a table, instruments that look like instruments, and a
figure at each that nods and bops on the beat. Arrivals walk in and pick
an instrument up. The banter appears as captions. Each agent is named by
its name and the model it runs on, as text. The watcher hears the hum,
then the rhythm, then the band coming in on a beat, and later the band
changing when the person hums again. A waveform or a bar counter may be a
corner detail. It is a stage, not a dashboard.

### The acts of the story

Each is a declared act of the jam's own definition. The table is plan
021's. The jam's definition is not written yet, so the names and what each
records are the plan's, not a tested design.

| Act | Who | What the room records |
|---|---|---|
| `sing` | the person | A theme: a MIDI-equivalent encoding of pitch and timing, transcribed from the microphone, with the digest of the audio beside it. Nothing of the audio itself is authoritative |
| `interpret` | the first agent (synth) | The interpretation of the latest theme: key, tempo, time signature, the bar grid, and the quantized theme. Others come in on this, not on the raw transcription. One interpretation per theme, in force until the next `sing` |
| `take` | an arriving agent | A claim on an instrument, in arrival order. An instrument is an exclusive commitment |
| `pattern` | any player | A pattern per bar or per phrase, bound to the interpretation it follows and the bar it starts at. This is what the live layer renders |
| `solo` | a lead | A claimed stretch of bars; one solo at a time |
| `say` | anyone | Banter, bounded in length |
| `release` | a player | Gives the instrument up |

The rules are the room's own: the instrument order, who may `interpret`,
how long a solo may run. A drummer who tries to `interpret` is refused, and
the demonstration shows that once. Section 7 says what main records of a
refusal.

Plan 021 does not say whether the rhythm phrase is a second `sing` or a
part of one theme. This note treats each phrase the person makes as a
`sing` act with its transcription, and lists the question in section 11.

### Where the tune is understood

Two places can turn singing into notes. Spike J0 decides between them.

1. **In the page.** The browser transcribes the hum into pitch and
   timing. The page shows the transcription, and the person can sing again.
   The room records the transcription as the `sing` fact. No audio leaves
   the person's machine.
2. **In the room.** The microphone audio goes to the synth agent, which
   uses a model that takes audio. The agent's `interpret` then carries
   both the transcription and the interpretation. It is slower, and how
   well a general model transcribes rough singing is not known.

Either way the `interpret` act is the synth's. Spike J0's provisional
recommendation is the first place (section 10).

## 2. The timing model

*Revision 6: kept. The rule is unchanged. The terms are now those of main:
an entry, its position `seq` and its `time`. Statements that named a
landed file now name the act that plan 021 gives. The table at the end of
this section walks each statement.*

### Three clocks

1. **The audio clock.** Local to each listener and exact to the sample.
   Web Audio schedules against it.
2. **The musical clock.** Bars and beats, computed from the historical
   tempo segments and a recorded transport start.
3. **The commit clock.** The room's history. Every entry has a position,
   `seq`, and a `time`: the reading of the service's clock at its commit
   (`Entry`, in `packages/contract/src/entry.ts`).

### The rule

A recorded change takes effect at **the first bar boundary at least one
lookahead after its entry's time**. Each transport run starts with one
entry that is its origin: the `seq` and the `time` of that entry record
the origin of bar 1. In plan 021's story that entry is the first
`interpret`, which fixes the tempo, the time signature and the bar grid.
A later `interpret` does not erase the run. It is a change like any other
and takes effect at a bar boundary under this rule.

The run needs positive lookahead values in milliseconds before it starts.
Revision 5 kept them, with the tempo, the meter and the form, in a file
that the leader landed. Plan 021 gives the tempo and the meter to
`interpret` and names no home for the lookahead. Where it is recorded is
open (section 11). Four beats per bar is the meter for the numerical
examples below. A listener's local audio buffer is not a scheduling input.

Process the history in `seq` order. For each change, use the current
transport run and the lookahead configuration in effect at its entry's
time. Use the tempo schedule established by lower-sequence entries,
including scheduled segments that have not started yet. Find the first
boundary at or after `time + lookaheadMs` on that schedule and retain its
bar number as that change's effect bar. Resolve changes on the same bar in
`seq` order. A tempo or lookahead change uses the preceding configuration
to choose its own effect bar, then activates there. A tempo change starts
a new segment at that bar's existing boundary. It can change the duration
of future bars, but cannot renumber an earlier assigned effect bar or
rewrite an elapsed boundary. Retain the old segments and configurations;
never calculate earlier changes from the latest interpretation or a
listener's current settings.

The recorded inputs are the origin entry, the time of each entry and the
exact content of each `interpret` entry. Activation bars are derived by
this ordered calculation, not chosen from a listener's arrival time. Every
listener, and a later reader of the whole history, therefore assigns the
same effect bars. The eventual musical fixture must compare live
processing with a fresh read of the history across both a tempo change
and a lookahead change, including an already scheduled effect. This
calculation is the jam's own code. It adds no platform commission.

Listeners estimate their offset from the service's clock through the live
layer, in the usual round-trip way.

A listener that receives a change after its effect bar has already passed
plays it late. Its live audio differs briefly from other listeners'. The
record does not, and the stage client (section 6) renders the official mix.

### Structure is recorded, playing is live

The room does not need an act for each note. The interpretation is the
structure: the key, the tempo and the bar grid that everyone comes in on.
Each pattern is recorded once, for a bar or a phrase, and the live layer
plays it against the musical clock. Acts mark what changes: a new theme,
an arrival, a pattern, a solo, a player who leaves.

### Choose forms that tolerate latency

Call and response, trading, dub and modal vamps all tolerate a reply that
arrives one bar later; it sounds intended. Tight unison comping does not.
The jam uses the first kind. The story is itself a call and a response:
the person hums, and the band answers.

### What the numbers allow

**Historical measurements of the earlier model,** from the room-core and
sandbox-git spikes (EWR, warm). That runtime is parked. These are kept as
dated inputs to J1 and say nothing measured about a scope:

- an act with no diff: 68 to 73 ms p50;
- a proposal of a one-file change: 305 ms p50;
- a merge preview: about 210 ms; a landing: about 510 ms inside the
  Durable Object;
- the first container start after a deploy: 5.9 s; later starts 223 to
  675 ms.

**Not measured:** the time from a signed act to its entry on a deployed
scope, delivery of an entry to a listener, and the rate of entries with
several players. Nothing is deployed, so none of these can be measured
yet.

At 120 bpm a bar lasts 2 s. A one-bar lookahead for every act of the
story is a hypothesis. In plan 021 a pattern is one act and one entry, so
revision 5's second, longer path for a pattern (a push, a version, a check
and a landing) has no user in the first phrase. J1 measures the act path
on the deployed version actually used.

### What the story needs of the timing

**The band's answer starts on a beat.** Under the rule, the first pattern
of each player takes effect on a bar boundary, never between beats. With a
one-bar lookahead at 120 bpm that boundary is between 2 s and 4 s after
the pattern's entry (computed from the rule, not measured).

**The stated latency.** From the moment the person stops to the band's
first downbeat, the target is at most 4 bars, which is 8 s at 120 bpm.
This is a target for J1 and J2 to test, not a measurement. In the clip the
track follows the rhythm with no gap, so the target may be too slow to be
funny; that is for the owner (section 11). The time is the sum of five
steps:

| Step | Time | How taken |
|---|---|---|
| Transcribe the phrase in the page | 202 to 373 ms for a 4.2 s phrase | Spike J0, 5 observed runs under Node on a loaded machine, on one clip; not measured in a page |
| Record the `sing` act | Unknown | Not measured: nothing is deployed |
| The synth agent reads the theme and decides the interpretation | Unknown | Not measured: it is a model call; J2 records it |
| Record the `interpret` act and the first patterns | Unknown | Not measured |
| Wait for the bar boundary | 2 to 4 s at a one-bar lookahead | Computed from the rule |

## 3. What lives where

*Revision 6: kept. The split is unchanged: commitments and facts are
recorded, playing is live. The acts are plan 021's and the terms are
main's. The table at the end of this section walks each statement.*

### What the room is on main

A scope is one small record with its own history, items and definition
([docs/scopes.md](../docs/scopes.md)). A definition is data, pinned by
digest when the scope is created, and it never changes. The jam's room is
expected to be one scope of the kind `lane` under the jam's own
definition, created by a directory, with the membership scope of the same
repository saying who may act. This is expected, not shown: no definition
from outside `packages/lanes` has been created under the deployed wiring,
and the directory that creates lanes cannot be created yet.

### Room acts

- **The theme.** `sing` records one phrase as a MIDI-equivalent encoding:
  for each note its pitch as a MIDI number, its start and its length, and
  for a hit of a rhythm its time and a class. That is what J0's
  transcriptions hold; the fields are not designed yet. The digest of the
  audio is beside it. The audio is not in the record.
- **The interpretation.** `interpret` records the key, the tempo, the
  time signature, the bar grid and the quantized theme. It names the theme
  it interprets. One is in force at a time.
- **Instruments.** `take` claims one instrument for one player, and the
  claim is exclusive. `release` gives it up. The order of arrival decides
  which instrument is next.
- **Patterns.** `pattern` records one player's bar or phrase. It names the
  interpretation it follows.
- **Solos.** `solo` claims a stretch of bars for a lead. A second solo
  over a held stretch is refused.
- **Banter.** `say` records a short text.
- **The rules.** They are the jam's definition. A different instrument
  order or solo length is a different definition with a different digest,
  for another room. Revision 5 changed house rules during a session by an
  approved landing of a policy file; a scope's definition does not change,
  so that is not available on main (section 7).

### What the record holds

Revision 5 kept the record in the files of a repository. Plan 021 makes
each of those things a fact in the room's history.

| Revision 5 | What it held | In revision 6 |
|---|---|---|
| `song.json` | key, tempo, meter, form and lookahead | The `interpret` entries hold key, tempo, meter and bar grid. The lookahead's home is open. There is no agreed form |
| `parts/<part>/*.json` | patterns, voicings, loop choices, sample references | The `pattern` entries |
| `samples/<pack>/**` and `descriptors.json` | audio, its provenance and descriptors | Set aside (section 4) |
| `takes/<player>/<section>.json` | a player's notes for one section | The `pattern` entries are the recorded performance. Whether a solo is recorded as patterns or played live only is open (section 11) |
| `.artroom/policy.json` | act declarations and house rules | The jam's definition, as data |

The transport origin is an entry of the history, the first `interpret`,
rather than a mutable start-time field. A later reader retains each run's
origin and the activation bars derived in section 2.

Each player signs their own patterns, so the recording is attributed and
signed part by part. The person signs the theme. Nothing in the record is
authored on anyone's behalf by the stage. If the tune were understood in
the room and not in the page (section 1), the transcription would be the
synth agent's statement and not the person's; that is one more reason for
the first place.

### The live layer

A separate Durable Object in the jam app's own Worker, using WebSocket
hibernation. It carries the playback of a transcription before it is
recorded, notes as they sound, the cues that make a figure bop, per-part
energy and density figures, and clock-offset pings. Every message carries
musical time. Under the first place of section 1 it carries no microphone
audio.

The rule: **nothing in the live layer is authoritative.** Anything that
matters becomes an act. Other systems carry copies, never authority.

The live layer is part of the jam app, not of Artroom. It needs no change
to the platform.

### Committing files from a browser or an agent

Revision 5 had each player push pattern and take files to a workspace fork
with isomorphic-git (MIT), then record version and landing acts. Plan
021's first phrase needs no file and no push: a pattern is one signed act.
This path is set aside, not deleted from the design. On main a hold
derives the records of a workspace, and no fork and no token exists at a
real host.

## 5. The agents

*Revision 6: rewritten. It replaces "The agent harness".*

### Four players, in order

The first agent to arrive is the synth and makes the interpretation. The
second takes the bass, the third the drums, and the fourth a lead. Each
has its own prompt and its own key.

### What a player perceives

Perception is symbolic. No player hears audio, unless spike J0 ends by
choosing the second place of section 1 for the synth. Before each phrase a
player receives:

- the theme and the interpretation in force: key, tempo, time signature
  and the quantized tune;
- the bar and beat position;
- the last bars of every part, as the recorded patterns;
- who holds each instrument and the solo;
- the recent banter.

### Two loops per agent

1. **The phrase loop** writes the next pattern before it is due and
   records it with `pattern`. It may be a small model, or an algorithm
   whose parameters (density, register, rhythm) the agent steers.
2. **The bandmate loop** is slower. It decides when to `take`, `solo`,
   `say` or `release`.

A model's response time is then one more lookahead. Notes are not
generated in real time.

### Connected directly

Each player is a long-lived agent. It holds a WebSocket to the live layer,
which is the jam's own. It follows the room through the scope's `stream`
route, which sends the scope's head after each commit as lines of JSON,
and then reads the new entries. A read needs a read session, which lasts
at most ten minutes and is issued to a device key of an active member.
Revision 5 named a WebSocket `watch` route and a `subscribe` route; they
were the earlier model's and main has neither.

Cloudflare's
[Agents SDK](https://developers.cloudflare.com/agents/runtime/communication/websockets/)
supports persistent agents and WebSocket connections and remains a
candidate. This note does not adopt a harness.

Each agent is a member of the repository with its own key and signs its
own acts, so every act is attributed to that player. Plan 021 says a
player's grant is limited to its instrument. Section 7, gap P3, says what
main has in place of that.

### Rate

At 120 bpm a four-bar phrase lasts 8 s, so each agent's phrase loop calls
a model about 7.5 times a minute. Four agents make about 30 calls a
minute, plus the slower bandmate loops (computed, not measured). What a
minute of the band costs to run is not estimated here; spike J2 records
it.

## 6. The person, the microphone and the stage

*Revision 6: rewritten. It replaces "The human, the MIDI keyboard and the
stage". The MIDI keyboard is set aside: the person's instrument is their
voice.*

- **Input.** The page reads the microphone. Under spike J0's provisional
  recommendation the page transcribes the phrase itself, and the audio
  stays on the person's machine.
- **Checking.** The page shows the transcription and plays it back. The
  person sings again if it is wrong. Only then does the page record the
  `sing` act.
- **The person's acts.** The person signs `sing` and `say` with the
  browser's key.
- **Monitoring.** The person hears the band scheduled locally against the
  shared musical clock.
- **The stage** is one listener that draws the room and renders the
  official mix for the video. It draws from the record: an instrument for
  each `take`, a figure that bops on the interpretation's beat, a caption
  for each `say`. It renders sound from recorded patterns, not from
  captured audio, so the session can be rendered again from the history.
  A part that was played live and never recorded is a reported gap. The
  hum itself is not in the record, so the video's sound of the person
  comes from the recording of the session, not from the room.
- **Audio.** Web Audio synthesis. The audio engine of our own earlier
  dubspace demonstration (MIT) is a candidate to reuse.
- **Visuals come after J2** has something to animate.

## 7. Where the room is seen and heard

*Revision 6: rewritten.*

If the room only keeps a history, the demonstration does not show Artroom.
Four moments make it visible:

1. **A refused interpretation.** The drummer tries to `interpret`. The
   room refuses, with the name of the guard that failed, and the stage
   shows it once. On main the refusal is an answer to the caller and
   writes nothing (gap P4).
2. **An arrival.** A new agent walks in and gets the next instrument in
   the order. A second claim on a held instrument is refused.
3. **A new theme.** The person hums again. The synth's `interpret` is
   recorded, and the band changes key and tempo at one bar boundary that
   every listener computes alike.
4. **The replay.** After the session, `artroom-replay` checks the room's
   history, and the stage renders the whole jam again from it.

Revision 5's second and third moments are dropped. A key change that
reopened every `in-key` check belonged to the sample and checker design.
A house rule that changed during the session is not possible in a scope
whose definition is fixed.

### Load the jam puts on the record

A scope processes one input at a time. Four players that each record one
pattern per four-bar phrase at 120 bpm write 30 entries a minute; one
pattern per bar is 120 a minute (computed). The rate a deployed scope
sustains is not measured. J1 measures it, and the phrase length is set
from the result.

A scope has a budget of entries: 100,000 under main's temporary bounds.
At 30 entries a minute that is about 55 hours, and at 120 a minute about
14 hours (computed from the temporary bound). Plan 021 says the band may
go on "as long as they like" at no cost in the record. That holds for a
session of hours and not without limit.

Plan 021 says the jam needs "nothing new beyond the I3 scope". That is a

missing or not shown. Each is a finding for a separate request in

## 8. Risks

*Revision 6: rewritten.*

- **The playback may not be the tune.** Whether a synth playing a
  transcription is recognizably what the person hummed is not known. Spike
  J0 has counts and timings on one clip and no judgment. Legato humming
  and a noisy room are untested.
- **Musicianship is unproven.** Agents that write patterns from symbolic
  perception may sound aimless. Spike J2 tests it. The fallback is agents
  steering parameterised generators rather than writing notes. The
  coordination would still be real; the music would be simpler.
- **The gap before the band comes in.** In the clip the track follows at
  once. The jam's answer waits for a transcription, a model call, several
  acts and a bar boundary. If the gap is long, the joke is lost. J1 and J2
  measure the parts.
- **It must be funny.** The story is a joke with a shape. A correct,
  dull performance fails it. Only a person can judge that.
- **The platform is not there yet.** Section 7 lists nine parts the jam
  needs and main lacks. J0 and J2 need none of them; J1 needs the first
  two.
- **What is heard in the video.** Dependencies of the jam need only a
  licence compatible with Apache 2.0. The tunes heard in the video are
  content, so they are our own: hummed by us and played by our synths.
  The reference clip is an excerpt of a published sketch. It is local test
  material for J0, it is not committed, and it is not shown or played in
  the demonstration.
- **A person's voice.** Under the first place of section 1 the audio
  stays on the person's machine and only its digest is recorded. Sending
  audio to a hosted model sends it off the machine and needs the owner's
  go-ahead.
- **Schedule.** The original plan named a first gate on 2026-10-05 and a
  submission on 2026-10-13. Those dates are from 2026-10-01 and this
  revision did not check them. The jam is built in its own repository and
  takes no core lane, but it competes for planning and review attention.

Revision 5's risk of failed repository and fork creations during a
recording is dated: it was measured on the earlier model, and the first
phrase creates no fork.

## 9. A separate application, and how applications are distributed

*Revision 6: the boundary is kept. The parts of an application, the
installation steps and the checker finding are rewritten against main.*

The jam is built in its own repository, `~/play/artroom-jam`, published at
<https://github.com/generalbusiness-ai/artroom-jam>. Artroom never depends
on it. It uses Artroom the way any outside application would, so it shows
where the boundary between the platform and an application really is.

### What an application consists of

| Part | In the jam | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| A definition | the jam's item types, acts and guards | data, pinned by its digest |
| The application Worker | static pages, the stage, the live layer's Durable Object | a Worker in the application owner's account |
| Agents | the players | long-lived agents, each a member with its own key |
| A setup script | creates the room and invites the players | a script that uses only the published client |

Revision 5 also listed validators: a sample checker and an `in-key` check.
The new story has neither.

### What it may depend on

Only published packages and the URL of a deployed service. No imports
from the Artroom repository's source paths, and no workspace links. The
test is simple: the jam's lockfile resolves every Artroom package from a
registry. Today the packages an application would use are the contract
types and the client handle. None is published (gap P7).

### Installing

Revision 5 listed three steps of the earlier model: found a room with a
new repository, land a template with a policy file, and invite the
players. On main the steps are expected to be: found a directory, giving
the jam's definition among its `definitions`; have it create the room
under that definition's digest; and invite each player through the
membership scope. None of this can be done on main today (gap P2), and
this note does not claim the steps are right until they have been run.

An application has no file to land in order to exist: its definition is
pinned when its room is created. An upgrade is then a new definition and
a new room. How a running room moves to a new definition is not a
question this note answers.

### The first finding, dated

Revision 2 found that the earlier room reached a checker over a service
binding fixed in the platform's own deployment, so an application's
checker could not be reached without redeploying the platform. That
finding was true of main at `a42c4d83`. It is dated, not withdrawn: on
main now the checkers package holds the pure parts of the checker service
and has no runner, no storage and no Worker, so how an application's
checker is reached is not yet a question with an answer. Plan 021's story
has no checker, so the jam does not wait on it.

### Other questions the jam settles

1. **Packages.** Their publication and scope name.
2. **Transport.** The client's `httpTransport` uses the HTTP routes, so a
   Worker in another account needs no service binding. To be confirmed on
   a deployment.
3. **A browser's key.** The person signs in the page. How a person's
   device key is made and kept is the application delivery's.
4. **Versions.** The application pins the contract package's version and
   its own definition's digest. `declaredHandle` answers a handle only
   when the room pins that digest.
5. **Where the application's own work is tracked.** In an Artroom
   repository for the jam, once Artroom can host one. Platform findings
   are filed in Artroom's workroom as requests.

Revision 5's questions about reading a proposal's files and about the
commit helper's workspace token belonged to the file path that section 3
sets aside.

### Starting development

Builder judges, on evidence and against a concrete first task, when the
platform is ready for it. J0 and J2 need no platform and are that work
now. The first task that needs the platform is to create a room under the
jam's definition and record a `sing`, an `interpret` and a `take` in it.
It waits on gaps P1 and P2. Before then, the jam's definition can be
written as data. Checking it needs the platform's validator,
`validateDefinition`. Until the packages are published that check cannot
be run from the jam's repository without importing platform source, which
the acceptance criterion forbids.

### J0: hum to theme, first half, 2026-10-05

**Status.** The measurements below are done. The judgment is not, because
the judge is a person listening and nobody has listened. The spike does
not yet answer its question. The source is the spike's note in the jam
repository, `notes/2026-10-05-j0-hum-to-theme.md`, which is uncommitted
there.

**What ran.** Two ways to transcribe in the page, on one machine, with no
audio leaving it:

- path A: an open audio-to-MIDI model, Basic Pitch 1.0.1 (Apache-2.0),
  run under Node on the CPU with the same JavaScript a page would load;
- path B: a plain-JavaScript pitch tracker with no model, the jam's own
  code, which splits notes on silence and on pitch jumps.

The material is one clip: the first seconds of the reference clip, cut
into a 4.2 s tune, a 4.2 s rhythm and an 11.2 s band excerpt.

**What was found.** Every figure is from observed runs on that one clip.

| Measure | Path B, pitch tracker | Path A, the model |
|---|---|---|
| Notes found in the hummed tune (about 8 syllables) | 8 | 47 as the model gave them, 24 of which start before 3.3 s; 11 after the spike's own reduction to one voice |
| Time to transcribe 4.2 s of audio | 202 to 373 ms (5 calls in one process) | 2.6 to 2.8 s (3 processes, two calls each) |
| What a page would download | about 6 KB gzip | about 0.74 MB gzip |

- The two paths agree on the pitch of every syllable that both found,
  within one semitone, and on the onset times, within about 0.1 s. The
  model split each syllable into fragments.
- Path B's eight notes are MIDI 45, 50, 56, 56, 56, 52, 58 and 51, a range
  of 13 semitones.
- For the rhythm, the onset detector found 18 hits in 4.2 s with a median
  gap of 0.25 s, which is 4.1 hits a second. It took 17 to 18 ms (5 calls).
  It sorted the hits into 5 low and 13 high; no reference labels exist, so
  the classes are not checked.
- The band excerpt has 39 detected onsets with a median gap of 0.2496 s.
  The spike could not read a melody from it, because it is polyphonic.

**Limits, as the spike states them.**

- One clip, one singer, one take, with clear gaps between syllables.
  Humming with slurs between notes, or in a noisy room, is untested.
- Two of path B's parameters were set after looking at this clip. Without
  one of them it finds 9 notes.
- Pitch accuracy is not measured; there is no ground truth. "Same pitch"
  means that the two paths agree.
- The machine was shared and loaded, so the timings are noisy. Path A was
  timed on the CPU under Node, not in a page. No browser timing was
  measured for either path.
- Download sizes are file sizes and `gzip -9`, not a network measurement.
- The microphone, the harness page's buttons and its playback were not
  run.

**What is still owed.**

- **The judgment.** The owner listens to four comparison files, each the
  original followed by the playback, and says whether the playback is
  recognizably the tune and the rhythm.
- **Five phrases by two people.** The done-when asks for at least five
  real hummed phrases by two people. The spike has one clip from one take.
- **A hosted audio model.** None was run. Calling one sends the audio off
  the machine, and the test clip is an excerpt of a published sketch, so
  it needs the owner's go-ahead first. The second half of the question,
  "if not, which model, and how fast", is therefore not done.

**Provisional recommendation.** Transcribe in the page with path B, with
a "sing again" button. It is provisional until the owner has listened. If
the playback is not recognizable, the spike's next step is a model called
from an agent, not a larger model in the page, because path A did no
better on pitch.

**One finding for plan 021.** The plan says a pitch tracker alone gives
pitch without onsets. On this clip, splitting on silence and on pitch
jumps gave onsets as well, because quiet gaps separate the syllables.

### J1: commit to bar

**When.** When commit-to-effect uncertainty blocks the musical
integration, against the deployed version that the application will use.
It cannot run before gaps P1 and P2 are closed. Record that version and
the digest of the jam's definition.

**Measured,** with four simulated players at 120 bpm, reporting sample
counts, p50 and p99, retries, and cold or warm state:

- (a) a single act, from the signed intent to a listener holding the
  entry, through the `stream` route and a read;
- (b) the rate of entries when every player records a pattern each phrase,
  and then each bar;
- (c) the error of a listener's offset estimate against the service's
  clock;
- (d) from the person's stop to the band's first downbeat, with the
  agents' model calls replaced by a fixed delay that the run states.

**Claims:**

- (a) p99 is at most 1.5 s, so a one-bar lookahead at 120 bpm holds;
- (c) the offset error is at most 20 ms p99;
- (d) the first downbeat is at most 4 bars (8 s) after the stop.

**Stop rule.** If (a) fails, the tested one-bar demonstration stops and
the finding is reported. A revised mode needs its own stated timing
expectations; a failed claim is not renamed as success. If only (b) fails
at one pattern a bar, patterns are recorded per phrase.

**Kept from revision 5:** items (a) and (c) with their claims, the stop
rule, and the rule that the transport measured is named. **Dropped:** the
pattern change from push to a landing receipt with its check, and its
4-bar claim, because a pattern is now one act; the run with eight players,
because the story has four; and the separate `subscribe` run, because main
has no such route.

### J2: riffing

**When.** After J0 has a theme to give it. It needs no room: a local
harness with a simulated musical clock and the perception of section 5.

**Set-up,** frozen before the run: a theme as J0 produces it; four agents
in the order synth, bass, drums, lead, each with its own prompt; the synth
interprets the theme and each player produces patterns on it; then a
second theme, which the band picks up at a bar boundary. Output is the
patterns as recorded and a rendered recording.

**Claims:**

- at least 95% of patterns are delivered before their start bar;
- the change to the second theme happens on one bar boundary for every
  player;
- the time of each model call, the calls per minute and what the run cost
  are recorded.

**Judgement.** Hugh listens to the recording and answers one question: is
it worth hearing? The answer is recorded with the audio.

**Stop rule.** A budget of two revisions to the harness and prompts. If
the answer is still no, the proposed musical demonstration stops, or
continues only in the fallback mode of section 8. Missing the timing
claims is reported separately from the musical judgment.

**Kept from revision 5:** the local harness, the two loops, the delivery
claim, the recorded cost, Hugh's judgment and the stop rule. **Dropped:**
the two-chord vamp at 100 bpm and the form of trading fours, because the
theme and the interpretation now give the key, the tempo and the shape;
the claims about solo turns and an ending on a named bar, because the
story names no form; and the optional run with a MIDI keyboard, because
the person's instrument is their voice.
